    5. Data reduction¶

    Depth: L2 · Reading time: ~11 min · Last verified: 2026-11-01 Prerequisites: Inside an SSD · Performance and economics metrics

    TL;DR¶

    • Data reduction lets one physical terabyte hold several logical ones. Four techniques stack: pattern removal (zeros and fills), deduplication (identical chunks stored once), similarity reduction (near-identical chunks stored as differences) and compression. The data-reduction ratio (DRR) is logical data written ÷ physical capacity consumed.
    • The ratio belongs to the data, not the array. VDI reaches 12–20:1, SQL Server 2–3:1, and JPEGs, encrypted volumes and most AI data sit near 1:1. The least reducible slice dominates a blend.
    • "Total efficiency" is not data reduction. Counting thin provisioning and snapshots multiplies a headline ratio with no change in the data. Pure Storage's own datasheet separates ~5:1 average reduction from ~10:1 "total efficiency" that includes thin provisioning1.
    • Guarantees transfer risk; the fine print sets the price. Dell (6:1, PowerStore Gen 3), NetApp (4:1 SAN), HPE (4:1) and Everpure (workload-based Right-Size Guarantee) mostly cover "reducible data" only and pay out in capacity.
    • AI is the hard case and the new battleground. Images, tokens and BF16 checkpoints barely shrink with generic tools. Similarity reduction (VAST, WEKA, Everpure's Purity DeepReduce) and format-aware compression are the attempted fixes.

    Why this matters for Everpure strategy¶

    All-flash arrays beat disk in primary storage partly through data reduction: dividing flash's higher raw $/TB by 4–5:1 closed much of the gap per effective terabyte. Everpure still quotes FlashArray in effective capacity, and Evergreen//One is billed on effective used capacity, measured before data reduction2. DRR is therefore a margin line. When a fleet reduces less than sizing assumed, someone pays for extra DirectFlash Modules, and under a guarantee or subscription that is the vendor.

    AI shifts the mix. VMs and databases reduce well; images, tokens and checkpoints do not. In AI deals raw $/TB, density and power matter more, which is where DirectFlash competes (Chapter 26), and near-duplicate reduction becomes a differentiator. VAST built its pitch on it; Everpure answered with DeepReduce on FlashBlade in 2026. A strategy team must separate physics from accounting.

    Core concepts¶

    Four techniques, one ratio¶

    DRR = logical bytes written by hosts ÷ physical bytes consumed, measured after reduction and before RAID or erasure-coding overhead, which sits elsewhere in the raw-to-effective waterfall (Chapter 4). Savings are 1 − 1/DRR: 2:1 saves 50%, 5:1 saves 80% and 10:1 saves 90%. Returns diminish: doubling 5:1 to 10:1 saves only ten more points, so compare percentages, not ratios.

    The pipeline runs cheapest first:

    flowchart LR
    W["Host write"] --> NV["Persist in NVRAM, ack host"]
    NV --> P["Pattern removal: zeros, fills"]
    P --> D["Dedupe: chunk, hash, index lookup"]
    D -->|"duplicate"| M["Store pointer only"]
    D -->|"unique"| S["Similarity lookup"]
    S -->|"near match"| DL["Encode delta vs reference"]
    S -->|"no match"| C["Compress, LZ4 or zstd"]
    DL --> PK["Pack variable-size output into log segments"]
    C --> PK
    PK --> F["Write to flash with RAID or EC"]
    F -.->|"later, in background"| DR["Heavier recompression, background dedupe"]

    Figure 5.1 — Reduction is a pipeline from cheap to expensive, and where the host acknowledgement sits relative to it is the inline-versus-post-process choice.

    Deduplication: chunk, fingerprint, look up¶

    Deduplication cuts data into chunks, hashes each into a fingerprint and checks an index. A hit stores only a pointer; a miss writes the chunk and indexes it.

    sequenceDiagram
    participant H as Host
    participant C as Controller
    participant I as Fingerprint index
    participant F as Flash
    H->>C: WRITE 64 KB
    C->>C: split into chunks, fixed or content-defined
    C->>C: hash each chunk with SHA-256
    C->>I: look up each fingerprint
    alt fingerprint found
        I-->>C: existing physical address
        C->>I: increment reference count
    else new fingerprint
        I-->>C: miss
        C->>F: compress and write chunk
        C->>I: insert fingerprint and address
    end
    C-->>H: ACK

    Figure 5.2 — Every write becomes an index lookup, so the speed and size of the fingerprint index, not the hash, set the cost of deduplication.

    Fixed versus variable chunking. Fixed chunking cuts every 4 KB or 8 KB. It is cheap and aligns with block addresses, but one inserted byte shifts every later boundary and nothing matches. Content-defined chunking (CDC) cuts wherever a rolling hash hits a chosen pattern, so boundaries follow the content and re-synchronise after an insert. LBFS used Rabin fingerprints for this, and FastCDC's cheaper Gear hash runs 3–12× faster at about the same dedupe ratio3. Backup appliances use CDC because backup streams shift; primary arrays mostly use aligned chunks because databases overwrite pages in place. Purity on FlashArray is a hybrid: variable 4–32 KB chunks scanned at 512-byte granularity, which catches misaligned copies4.

    The index is the real problem. 1 PB of unique data in 8 KB chunks is 10¹⁵ ÷ 8,192 ≈ 1.2 × 10¹¹ chunks. At ~40 bytes per entry (32-byte SHA-256 plus address) the index is ~5 TB, far more than a controller pair's DRAM. Data Domain kept a Bloom filter in RAM to answer "definitely new" cheaply, laid chunks out in stream order and prefetched neighbouring fingerprints, removing 99% of index disk accesses5. Many primary arrays keep a partial index and treat dedupe as best-effort. VAST keeps reduction metadata in shared storage-class memory6 (Chapter 16). Larger chunks shrink the index but find fewer duplicates.

    Semiconductor bridge. The fingerprint index is the tag RAM of a huge cache where every write is a lookup, and the Bloom filter is its snoop filter. Collisions are not a practical risk: for 1.2 × 10¹¹ chunks and a 256-bit hash, n² ÷ 2²⁵⁷ ≈ 10⁻⁵⁵.

    Compression: redundancy inside the chunk¶

    LZ-family compressors replace repeated strings with back-references, then entropy-code. Speed trades against ratio: on the Silesia corpus LZ4 reaches ~2.1:1 at ~700 MB/s per core and zstd level 1 ~2.9:1 at ~510 MB/s, both decompressing above 1.5 GB/s7. Arrays run a fast codec inline and recompress cold data in the background (Purity's "deep reduction"4). Offload engines are the analogue of an SSD controller's LDPC block (Chapter 3): fixed-function logic wins on throughput per watt.

    Granularity is the subtle trade-off. 32 KB units compress better than 4 KB units, but a 4 KB random read must decompress the whole unit. Compressed output varies in size, so it is packed into log-structured segments and garbage-collected later, adding write amplification. That is why compression bolted onto update-in-place designs struggled and why Purity and VAST were log-structured from the start.

    Similarity reduction: storing only the difference¶

    One changed byte defeats exact-match deduplication. Similarity reduction uses a locality-sensitive hash so near-identical blocks collide. The first block with a hash becomes the reference; later matches are compressed with it as a dictionary, storing only differences8. VAST dedupes first, then applies compression or similarity, whichever saves more, while data migrates from the SCM buffer to QLC, off the write path9. Hash tables in shared SCM make the scope cluster-wide6.

    VAST's figures are company-stated: ~3:1 across its installed base in 202210, 8:1 on one quant firm's market data6, and a ~2:1 median in a later webinar11. Everpure's Purity DeepReduce is also similarity-based, cluster-wide and asynchronous12. It reached FlashBlade in February 2026, and at the end of September Everpure announced an always-on version for October. Everpure cites a ~2:1 median, measured on top of data that was already compressed, so it is incremental to conventional reduction rather than a total ratio1314. The costs: reading a delta block may also require its reference, and referenced blocks cannot be freed, which complicates garbage collection. Semiconductor bridge: this is video coding's P-frame, encoded as a difference from an I-frame.

    Inline versus post-process¶

    Inline reduction runs before data reaches flash: fewer NAND writes, no landing space, but CPU work in the write path, and some systems defer it under load, so the ratio can depend on load. Post-process lands data raw or lightly compressed and reduces later: lowest write latency and room for heavier algorithms, but it needs landing capacity and the ratio lags ingest. Most systems are hybrids that acknowledge from NVRAM, reduce lightly inline and work harder in the background. Purity's deep reduction4, ONTAP's inline-plus-background deduplication and its temperature-sensitive efficiency for cold data15, VAST's reduction at migration9 and WEKA's background similarity reduction16 all follow this pattern. Ask what the ratio is in migration week versus month three.

    Pattern removal, thin provisioning and the "total efficiency" trick¶

    Pattern removal records all-zero or repeating-pattern blocks as metadata4. The saving is real, but zero-filled thick VM disks and preallocated database files "reduce" without limit, so some VM-estate ratios are partly zeros.

    Thin provisioning allocates capacity only on write. That is capacity efficiency, not data reduction, as Pure Storage's own blog argues: 8 TB on a 10 TB thin LUN "saves" 20%, on a 100 TB LUN 92%17. Snapshots and clones are metadata copies (Chapter 6); counting each at full logical size inflates further.

    Worked example: hosts write 100 TB that reduces 3:1, so 33.3 TB physical. Provisioned at 400 TB, "total efficiency" is 400 ÷ 33.3 = 12:1. Add seven daily snapshots counted at 100 TB each, with 1% daily change (7 TB written, 2.3 TB physical): (400 + 700) ÷ 35.6 ≈ 31:1. The data has not changed.

    xychart-beta
    title "Illustrative: same 100 TB of data, three headline ratios"
    x-axis ["Data reduction only", "Plus thin provisioning", "Plus 7 snapshots as copies"]
    y-axis "Reported ratio x to 1" 0 --> 35
    bar [3, 12, 31]

    Figure 5.3 — Illustrative worked example: counting thin provisioning and snapshots turns a 3:1 reduction into a 31:1 headline with no change to the data.

    What different data reduces to¶

    Data type Typical reduction Why Source
    SQL Server databases 2.2–2.9:1 Page compression; little duplication unless copies are present Everpure guidance based on Pure1 telemetry18
    Oracle databases 3.5–4.1:1 Same, with more compressible block formats Everpure guidance18
    Server VMs (VSI) 6–8:1 Common OS and application images deduplicate Everpure guidance18
    VDI 12–20+:1 Near-identical desktop images Everpure guidance18
    Already-compressed media and backups (JPEG, MP4, zstd Parquet) ~1.0–1.1:1 Already entropy-coded Engineering estimate
    Host-encrypted data 1.0:1 Ciphertext looks random Engineering fact
    AI training images (JPEG/PNG shards) ~1.0–1.1:1 Already compressed; duplicates are rare after curation Engineering estimate
    Tokenized text (uint16 shards) ~1.1–1.5:1 The tokenizer has already removed much of the redundancy Author estimate; raw text gets ~1.9:1 with zstd19
    BF16 checkpoints, format-aware lossless ~1.5:1 Skewed exponent bits; mantissas look random ZipNN, 33% savings20
    Successive dense checkpoints, dedupe ~1:1 Every weight changes each step; deltas compress better ZipNN20

    Table 5.1 — Reduction is a property of the data. AI data is near the bottom of the range unless the reduction is format-aware.

    Blended ratios are harmonic, not arithmetic. Physical = Σ(logical share ÷ DRR). An estate of 80% VMs at 6:1 and 20% images at 1:1 needs 0.8/6 + 0.2/1 = 0.333 of its logical size: 3:1, not the 5:1 an average suggests.

    For AI, tokenizers are themselves compressors: raw English text compresses ~1.9:1 with zstd and token-ID packing alone ~2.25:119, so stored shards have little redundancy left. In BF16 weights, half the bits are exponent; a format-aware coder such as ZipNN exploits their skew to save ~33%, beating generic codecs20. The big AI wins sit in the application: Check-N-Run's incremental, quantized checkpoints cut capacity 2.5–8× for recommendation models21, but that is lossy, app-side and invisible to array DRR (Chapter 31).

    Interrogating a vendor DRR claim¶

    flowchart TD
    A["Vendor quotes X to 1"] --> B{"Includes thin provisioning, snapshots or clones?"}
    B -->|"yes"| B1["Ask for reduction-only ratio"]
    B -->|"no"| C{"Measured on your data or close peers?"}
    B1 --> C
    C -->|"no"| C1["Run assessment tool or POC on a data copy"]
    C -->|"yes"| D{"Any host-compressed or encrypted share?"}
    C1 --> D
    D -->|"yes"| D1["Carve it out at 1 to 1 and re-blend"]
    D -->|"no"| E{"Mean or median, and what spread?"}
    D1 --> E
    E --> F{"Guarantee covers your mix with capacity remedy?"}
    F -->|"yes"| G["Size at guaranteed ratio plus headroom"]
    F -->|"no"| H["Size at your own blended ratio"]

    Figure 5.4 — A defensible sizing ratio comes from stripping accounting effects, carving out incompressible data, and only then deciding how much to trust the vendor's guarantee.

    The six questions to put to any vendor:

    1. What is in the numerator and denominator? Written or provisioned capacity? Zero-filled, snapshot or clone space?
    2. Whose data? Which workload, median not mean, and the 25th-percentile customer.
    3. How much of mine is already compressed or encrypted? Backups, media, Parquet, AI shards, host-encrypted volumes.
    4. Inline or post-process, and under load? The ratio in migration week one and at 80% utilisation.
    5. What does reduction cost? Latency and throughput with it on and off, and index DRAM or SCM per petabyte.
    6. What does the guarantee promise? Scope ("reducible data"), measurement point, remedy (capacity or refund) and duration.

    Worked example: $/effective TB¶

    A buyer needs 1,000 TB logical: 400 TB databases, 400 TB VMs, 200 TB AI images and checkpoints. Prices are illustrative.

    Vendor X Vendor Y
    Price per usable TB $600 $450
    Headline DRR → headline $/effective TB 5:1 → $120 3:1 → $150
    Assumed DRR: databases / VMs / AI 2.5 / 6 / 1.2 2.2 / 4.5 / 1.1
    Physical needed (Σ logical ÷ DRR) 160 + 66.7 + 166.7 = 393 TB 181.8 + 88.9 + 181.8 = 453 TB
    Blended DRR 2.54:1 2.21:1
    Usable bought (+20% headroom) 472 TB → $283k 543 TB → $244k
    Realistic $/effective TB $283 $244

    Table 5.2 — Illustrative worked example: the headline makes X 20% cheaper, but on this data mix X costs 16% more.

    The guarantee changes the answer. If X's 5:1 guarantee covered the 800 TB of databases and VMs, the shortfall (226.7 − 160 = 66.7 TB) would be free capacity worth ~$40k, bringing X to ~$243 per effective TB, level with Y. A guarantee's scope is worth as much as its ratio.

    Table 5.3 — Everyone reduces data. The real differences are scope (per volume versus global), similarity versus exact matching, and how carefully each guarantee is written.

    Guarantee mechanics matter more than headlines. Dell's PowerStore terms exclude host-compressed and host-encrypted data, require reduction to stay on, and accept claims only once half the physical capacity is written28. In Dell's favour, its 6:1 needs no assessment and no signed agreement23. HPE's remedy (expertise, capacity or other compensation) is at its discretion27. Everpure inverts the fixed-ratio promise: it sizes each customer's workloads and guarantees the resulting effective capacity, under a signed agreement2229. For mixed or AI-heavy estates that fits the data better than a banner ratio. The catch is that the vendor sets the sizing ratio, and a conservative sizer can make any guarantee safe, so the buyer should compare the sized capacity, not the existence of a guarantee.

    Steelman VAST. Global similarity finds savings exact-match dedupe misses, and running it at migration keeps it off the latency path. FlashArray's strength is mature inline reduction on well-understood block workloads. Putting DeepReduce on FlashBlade concedes that similarity matters for unstructured and AI data. Options a strategy team might evaluate, based on public information: extending similarity reduction to FlashBlade//EXA data nodes, and format-aware codecs for checkpoints and KV cache (Chapter 42, Chapter 45).

    History and trajectory¶

    timeline
    title Data reduction milestones
    2001 : LBFS popularises content-defined chunking
    2008 : Data Domain paper shows dedupe at disk speed
    2016 : Pure Storage Right-Size Guarantee : NetApp 4 to 1 AFF guarantee : FastCDC
    2018 : Pure Storage buys StorReduce
    2019 : VAST launches with similarity reduction
    2023 : WEKA 4.2 adds similarity-based data reduction
    2026 : DeepReduce on FlashBlade : DeepReduce always-on

    Figure 5.5 — Reduction moved from backup (2000s) to primary flash (2010s) to unstructured and AI data (2020s), and the guarantees followed it.

    Deduplication grew up in backup, where Data Domain showed it could run at disk speed5; flash's raw $/TB then made it a primary-storage feature. Guarantees followed in 2016 from Pure Storage30 and NetApp31. Pure bought StorReduce in 2018 for US$20.5 million in cash, net of cash acquired, to bring deduplication to cloud object storage32. VAST launched in 2019 with similarity reduction33. WEKA added post-process similarity reduction in its 4.2 release in 202316 and featured data reduction again in NeuralMesh 6, launched in July 202634. In 2026 Everpure followed on unstructured and AI data with DeepReduce on FlashBlade13. Next is format awareness: codecs that understand tensors, token streams and KV-cache blocks20.

    Conversation playbook¶

    Questions for a PM

    1. "What is the median and 25th-percentile DRR across the fleet for each workload, excluding thin provisioning and snapshots?" Strong: a telemetry distribution by workload, with "total efficiency" called out as a different metric.
    2. "How does the Right-Size Guarantee price AI datasets and checkpoints?" Strong: how near-1:1 data is carved out, historical payout, and how that cost is reserved.
    3. "Where does DeepReduce win against VAST similarity reduction, and where does it lose?" Strong: ratios on the same datasets, scope, and candour about EXA data nodes.

    Questions for an engineer

    1. "How big is the dedupe index per petabyte, and where does it live?" Strong: bytes per entry, placement (DRAM, NVRAM, flash) and miss behaviour.
    2. "Under what load does inline reduction defer, and how fast does post-process catch up?" Strong: thresholds, a backlog metric, worst-case landing capacity.
    3. "What is the read amplification of a 4 KB random read on compressed or similarity-encoded data?" Strong: compression unit size, reference-block reads, cache hit rate.

    Don't say this:

    • "Our arrays get 10:1." Say whether that is reduction or total efficiency, and for which workload.
    • "Dedupe works on AI data." Exact duplicates are rare after curation; gains come from similarity or format-aware coding.
    • "Higher ratio is always better." 5:1 to 10:1 saves ten more points and may cost latency.
    • "The guarantee covers us." Most exclude host-compressed and encrypted data and pay only in capacity.
    • "Encryption doesn't affect reduction." Host-side encryption drops it to 1:1; only array-side encryption after reduction preserves it.

    • DRR — logical written ÷ physical consumed.
    • Content-defined chunking — boundaries set by a rolling hash.
    • Similarity reduction — near-duplicates stored as deltas.
    • Total efficiency — DRR plus thin provisioning and snapshots.
    • Right-Size Guarantee — Everpure's workload-based capacity guarantee.
