    24. Architecture decision matrix¶

    Depth: L2 · Reading time: ~8 min · Last verified: 2026-11-01 Prerequisites: The storage stack · Scale-up dual-controller · Scale-out shared-nothing · DASE · Parallel file systems · Object storage · SDS and HCI

    TL;DR¶

    • Seven architectures cover almost every deal. Each has a home workload and a wall (Chapters 14–19).
    • Five questions decide most deals: how big, which protocol, latency or throughput, how large a failure domain the customer accepts, and who operates it at what cost. Vendor features come after.
    • The deepest split is latency-bound versus throughput-bound. Databases and VMs want sub-millisecond block; AI training and HPC want TB/s into one namespace.
    • AI is not one workload. Data loading, checkpointing, KV-cache serving and RAG land in different cells, so "AI storage" is a portfolio question.
    • The boundaries are blurring. Everyone now sells "unified" block, file and object, and Gartner's 2025 Magic Quadrant merged them into one category1. Underneath, the architectures, and their walls, have not converged.

    Why this matters for Everpure strategy¶

    Everpure sells three architectures: scale-up (FlashArray); a partitioned blade scale-out (FlashBlade//S and //E), which Everpure's engineers call "partitioned-writer", arguing that neither the shared-nothing nor the shared-everything label fits2 (Chapter 15); and a pNFS-style parallel design that splits metadata from data (FlashBlade//EXA, Chapter 17). It adds container-native SDS (Portworx) and Purity in public clouds. It sells no DASE system, because in EXA no compute tier reaches all media (Chapter 16), and no general-purpose HCI. A strategy team needs to know which cells Everpure covers natively, where it partners, and where rivals fit better by design, because discounting won't win those cells.

    The matrix also guards against category errors: an enterprise "AI storage" request is often mostly a capacity and governance problem.

Ratings are the atlas's judgment for typical 2026 deployments, not benchmarks; a strong product can beat its row. The SDS / HCI row rates HCI and container-native SDS; SDS sold as software for commodity servers (WEKA, Ceph, VAST's software-only route) is rated in the row of its architecture (Chapter 19).

    Figure 24.1 — Read the heatmap by row for an architecture's home turf and by column for which architectures compete for a workload. Most columns have two or three credible answers, not one.

    Where Everpure's products sit. FlashBlade//S and //E belong in the scale-out row. Namespace and write ownership are partitioned into "authorities" that run on blades, much like shared-nothing owners, while Purity manages DFM flash and NVRAM across the chassis (Chapter 15). Pooled media management softens the row's rebuild and hot-spot caveats but does not change its OLTP or archive cells. FlashBlade//EXA belongs in the parallel-file-system row, not DASE. Clients get a layout from the metadata core and then move data directly to data nodes, the Lustre MDS/OSS pattern (Chapter 17). Its stock pNFS client eases the row's "client friction" caveat, and its Purity metadata core is pitched at the small-file weakness (company-stated, Chapter 21). Its failure-domain story is weaker: data-node protection is not yet publicly documented.

    The five questions that decide a real deal¶

    Question What to ask Pushes toward
    Scale Capacity today and in three years; how many clients <~2 PB, tens of hosts → scale-up; multi-PB, thousands of clients → scale-out, DASE, parallel FS, object
    Protocol Block, NFS/SMB, POSIX shared-file, S3 Block → scale-up or cloud block; S3 → object; shared-file writes from many nodes → parallel FS
    Latency vs throughput p99 latency target, or aggregate GB/s Latency → fewest hops (scale-up); throughput → most parallel paths (parallel FS, DASE)
    Failure domain What may one fault take down, and how long is a rebuild? Small blast radius → many small arrays; wide EC → big shared pools
    Operating model and economics Who runs it, kernel clients allowed, capex vs service Thin team → appliance or cloud; HPC team → parallel FS; cost per TB → object/QLC

    Figure 24.2 — The first three questions narrow the matrix to one or two rows; the last two usually choose between them.

    Latency versus throughput is the deepest split, and it is physics. A 4 KB database write on a networked all-flash array completes in roughly 100–250 µs (Chapter 13). Each extra fabric hop or forwarded request adds tens of microseconds, so the latency winner is the design with the fewest hops between host and NVRAM. Throughput is the reverse problem. A 405B-parameter checkpoint is about 405e9 × 16 bytes ≈ 6.5 TB. At 20 GB/s (an illustrative single-array figure) the write takes 6.5e12 ÷ 20e9 ≈ 325 s. At the 877.5 GiB/s that 30 FlashBlade//EXA data nodes posted in MLPerf Storage v3.03 (≈ 942 GB/s), it takes ≈ 7 s. That gap is the whole case for parallel paths. The semiconductor bridge is the HBM-versus-DRAM trade: HBM wins on bandwidth by going wide, DDR wins on cost and capacity, and no single interface wins both.

    Failure domain costs money a spec sheet hides. A dual-controller array loses at most itself; a failed DASE enclosure or shared-nothing node puts a slice of a giant namespace into rebuild (Chapter 20).

    Justifying the contentious cells¶

    • Scale-up gets ● for RAG/vector. A vector database is a database. Its hot index lives in DRAM or on low-latency block, which arrays serve well. The source corpus belongs in object or file, which is why object also gets ●, helped by AWS making S3 Vectors generally available in December 2025 with up to two billion vectors per index4.
    • Shared-nothing and DASE get ◐, not ○, for OLTP, even though Chapters 15 and 16 list latency-critical block among their worst fits. Block SDS such as Dell PowerFlex runs production databases, and VAST added NVMe/TCP block in 20255. But every I/O still crosses a back-end hop, and few banks yet run tier-1 databases on either.
    • Object gets ◐ for training and checkpointing, although Chapter 18 lists sharded datasets and checkpoints among its best fits. Many labs stream shards from S3 through caching loaders and upload checkpoints asynchronously from local NVMe. The ◐ reflects synchronous checkpoint writes and small-file random access, which drops training to ○.
    • Parallel FS gets ◐ for KV cache. The bandwidth is there, and EXA led the v3.0 KV-cache read test3, but serving millions of small, latency-sensitive KV blocks is newer ground than streaming checkpoints (Chapter 32).
    • Cloud block gets ● for OLTP despite cost. io2 Block Express promises under 500 µs average at 16 KiB and 256K IOPS per volume6. The objection is price per IOPS, not fit.

    Figure 24.4 — Placement is the atlas's judgment: the commercial prize is the top-right quadrant, which DASE occupies and pNFS-style splits are trying to reach, so that is where the AI-era competition concentrates.

    Object sits low because self-run PB-scale object needs expertise; managed S3 would sit near cloud block. DASE scores well because stateless compute nodes make upgrades routine. The pNFS split (FlashBlade//EXA's design) sits between DASE and classic parallel file systems. The stock NFS client removes Lustre's kernel-module lifecycle, but customers still run an RDMA data fabric and, for EXA, source and operate the data-node servers (Chapter 17).

    Where the boundaries are blurring¶

    Three forces are blurring the rows. First, protocol unification: FlashArray gained S3 in 20257, VAST added block5, WEKA's NeuralMesh 6 unified file and object8, and HPE positions its Alletra Storage MP family as one platform for block (B10000) and file and object (X10000)9. Second, control-plane unification: Everpure's Enterprise Data Cloud10 and DDN's CORE layer over EXAScaler and Infinia11 present several architectures as one pool. Third, hybrids: FlashBlade//EXA is a parallel file system on a scale-out metadata engine (Chapter 17). NetApp's AFX is shared-media but documented for NAS and object, not block12.

    Test any "unified" claim against the matrix: which column was the protocol added for, and do its latency and failure domain match a native design?

    Table 24.5 — Everpure covers five of the seven rows natively, plus the SDS half of the SDS / HCI row with Portworx and the HCI half by partnership; DASE is the row it competes against rather than in.

    The steelman for VAST: one DASE system covers most of the heatmap's right half with one namespace and reduction domain, which suits neoclouds. For Dell and NetApp: a native product in nearly every row under one contract. Everpure's counter is one OS family (Purity) across FlashArray and FlashBlade, including EXA's metadata core though not its Linux data nodes, and one control plane (Pure1, Fusion), with media economics from DirectFlash (Chapter 26). Options a strategy team might evaluate, based on public information: closing the KV-cache cell faster (PureKVA for FlashBlade was announced in September 2026 for October availability17); using Portworx's Kubernetes VM-migration cell to pull through arrays; and keeping "unified" messaging behind per-workload proof points.

    History and trajectory¶

    Until about 2010 the matrix had three rows: SAN arrays, NAS filers and tape. Shared-nothing and object came with web-scale data, HCI with 2010s virtualization, DASE and pNFS revivals with GPUs. In 2025 the market started folding rows together: Gartner's new Enterprise Storage Platforms category1, NetApp AFX in October 202512, and DDN CORE in November 202511. The signal to watch in 2027 is whether buyers run one platform across columns, as CoreWeave's reported US$1.17B VAST agreement suggests for neoclouds18, or keep best-of-breed per column, as most enterprises still do.

    Conversation playbook¶

    Questions for a PM 1. "Which cells do we lose on architecture rather than price?" Strong answer: names them (shared-file-write HPC, multi-site geo-EC object) and a plan for each: partner, roadmap or walk away. 2. "Where does 'unified' help a buyer?" Strong answer: one control plane and support contract, with honesty about which protocol is native. 3. "What is our AI-inference story by cell?" Strong answer: separates KV cache (PureKVA) from RAG corpora (FlashBlade S3/NFS) from vector-DB block (FlashArray).

    Questions for an engineer 1. "What is the p99 penalty of block through a scale-out design?" Strong answer: the extra hop in microseconds, plus rebuild effects on tail latency. 2. "How big is the failure domain in EXA's data tier today?" Strong answer: states the current data-node protection scheme [VERIFY: current FlashBlade//EXA data-node protection] and rebuild time per node. 3. "Where does the decision tree break?" Strong answer: small-file AI datasets look throughput-bound but are metadata-bound (Chapter 21).

    Don't say this: - "One platform for every workload." No architecture rates ● in every column, including Everpure's. - "Object is just for archive." It is the default RAG and data-lake substrate. - "Parallel file systems are legacy HPC." They lead AI checkpointing benchmarks. - "DASE can't do block." It can; the question is latency and maturity. - "FlashBlade is Everpure's shared-everything answer to VAST." FlashBlade//S is a partitioned scale-out design and EXA a metadata/data split; neither is DASE.
