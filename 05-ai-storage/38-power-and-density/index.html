    38. Power, density and the AI datacenter¶

    Depth: L2 · Reading time: ~10 min · Last verified: 2026-11-01 Prerequisites: Media physics · Performance and economics metrics · Purity and DirectFlash · AI factories and neoclouds

    TL;DR¶

    • AI datacenters are power-limited, not chip-limited. NVIDIA, Microsoft, AWS, Meta and CoreWeave have all said so publicly. A site's grid connection caps its GPUs, so every storage watt is a watt that cannot run a GPU.
    • QLC flash beats disk about 2× per watt at the drive (~8.2 against ~4.4 TB/W for the best of each). At system level the gap depends on drive size and on what sits in the box. A 2022-era flash enclosure can lose to a modern disk JBOD.
    • Density is where flash pulls away. A rack of 245 TB QLC drives holds roughly 5× the raw petabytes of a rack of 44 TB disks. It also needs 2–3× the power per rack, which many halls cannot feed.
    • At grid prices, power savings never pay for the flash premium. Storing 100 PB for five years on QLC rather than disk saves about 1.2 GWh, or roughly $170K, against a media premium of $8–37M on Chapter 4's inputs, and more at index prices. Flash wins when bandwidth per TB is required or when NAND is bought at hyperscaler cost; turning freed watts into GPUs helps but rarely closes the gap on its own.
    • Disk still wins cold data at rest, on $/TB and on embodied carbon. HAMR roadmaps (100 TB by 2029) improve disk's TB/W every generation. Vendor power claims from both camps need system-level, same-basis checks.

    Why this matters for Everpure strategy¶

    "Flash replaces disk" used to be a cost-curve argument. With enterprise QLC at 15–23× disk per TB in 2026 (Chapter 2), it is now a power argument. Everpure (NYSE: P, formerly PSTG) has made that argument central. Its DirectFlash hyperscaler wins were explained in terms of footprint and power (Chapter 29), and it sells a contractual watts-per-TiB guarantee. If power stays the binding constraint for AI build-outs, a vendor with the best system-level TB/W has a demand driver that is independent of the NAND price cycle. If grid supply loosens, or HAMR closes the gap, the pitch falls back to $/TB, where disk wins.

    This chapter gives the arithmetic to tell which world a deal is in. It builds on the metrics in Chapter 4 and the module design in Chapter 26, and does not repeat them.

    Core concepts¶

    The site power cap is the budget everything else comes out of¶

    An AI campus starts from a fixed grid allocation, measured in megawatts and set years ahead by utilities and permits. GPUs are the revenue engine, so operators hand them as much of that allocation as possible. Networking, cooling overhead and storage share what is left. The leaders say this openly.

    At about 120 kW per GB200 NVL72 rack8, one GPU and its share of the rack draws roughly 1.7 kW. Chapter 35 works out storage at about 4% of a GPU row's power. Small, but not free.

    Semiconductor bridge: storage after the end of frequency scaling¶

    This is the move chip design made around 2005, when Dennard scaling ended. Once power density stopped falling with each node, clock frequency stopped being the goal. Designs became power-limited, and perf/W became the metric that sold chips. In a fixed-TDP SoC, watts saved in the uncore (I/O, fabric, memory controllers) become turbo headroom for the cores. Storage is the AI campus's uncore. Every watt it saves goes to the "cores", the GPUs, and Huang's framing makes tokens per watt the revenue metric. Storage vendors are being pulled into perf/W thinking the way CPU vendors were twenty years ago.

    TB per watt: what the datasheets actually say¶

    Figure 38.2 — At the drive, QLC is about 2× disk per watt; at system level, older flash enclosures and performance appliances can trail a modern disk JBOD. Data: data/ch38_tb_per_watt.csv

    Three readings matter. First, drive-level TB/W tracks capacity per device, because power per device barely changes. A nearline disk draws about 9.5–10 W whether it holds 30 or 44 TB910. A large QLC drive draws 25–30 W whether it holds 122 or 245 TB1110. Everpure's 75 TB module was stated at under 10 W12. Second, system overhead swamps drive differences. Controllers, fans, NICs and DRAM cut TB/W 2–10×. VAST's 1U DBox reaches about 1.9 TB/W before its CNodes13. A WD 102-bay JBOD of 22 TB disks reaches about 1.7 TB/W before its head server14; filled with 44 TB disks at similar watts it would reach about 3.5 TB/W (illustrative), ahead of the DBox. Everpure's FlashBlade//E (0.83 W/TB per expansion) and FlashBlade//S500 (1.3 W/TB) include their compute1516. Third, performance boxes sit in a different class. DDN's 2U AI400X3 is rated at 2,400 W17, about 0.2 usable TB/W on an implied ~500 TB [VERIFY: usable capacity per appliance]. It is built for GB/s per kW, not TB/W, and its TB/W depends on the drives fitted: the AI400X3M variant launched in June 2026 offers up to 2 PB usable per appliance18, so read the DDN bar as one configuration, not a ceiling.

    The lesson: compare systems at the same scope, watts basis and drive generation.

    Two caveats keep this honest. A full flash rack at ~32 kW exceeds the 10–15 kW many air-cooled halls feed, so operators spread it across racks. And deployed racks, such as Meta's ">10 PB", run well below the theoretical ceiling because of power, cooling, network ports and drive generation. Disk racks hit a different wall: floor loading. A rack of 742 nearline drives weighs well over a tonne.

    Table 38.3 — Illustrative: QLC halves the power and cuts racks 5×, but the ~$170K energy saving is two orders of magnitude smaller than the media premium.

    The media inputs are the same as Chapter 4's and are kind to flash. $302 against $22 per TB is a 13.7× premium, below the 22.6× index headline, because a 122 TB drive's street price per TB sits under the index's 30 TB QLC drive (about $504/TB in Q1 2026)25. At that index price the QLC media would cost ~$67M. Disk moved too: the same index put a 30 TB HDD at about $1,216 (~$40/TB) by Q3 202622, which lifts the HDD media line to ~$5.4M and leaves the 2026 premium at ~$35M. Every variant leaves the energy saving two orders of magnitude below the premium.

    The flash case has three escape routes from that table.

    1. Freed watts become GPUs. The 21.6 kW of IT power saved is about 13 GPU slots at 1.7 kW each. At CoreWeave's US$6.16 H100 list price per GPU-hour26, 13 GPUs would bill about $3.5M over five years. That is gross rental, before the GPUs' own capex, financing and operating cost, and it prices a GB200-class power slot at an H100 rate, so treat it as an upper bound. Chapter 4 values a freed kilowatt at an assumed $15,000 a year, which puts the slot at about $1.6M. Either figure beats the electricity saving many times over. Neither covers the ~$37M premium at 2026 NAND prices, and even the upper bound covers under half of the ~$8.5M premium at mid-2025 prices. Premium and freed watts both scale with capacity, so scale alone does not close the gap: this route works only alongside routes 2 or 3.

    2. Bandwidth per TB is required. Meta's argument for QLC is that disk bandwidth per TB keeps falling as drives grow27. Meta places QLC at workloads needing about 10 MB/s per TB. For 100 PB usable that means 1 TB/s. A nearline disk streams perhaps 250 MB/s at best and far less under mixed access. At an assumed 125 MB/s effective, you need about 8,000 drives instead of 3,030, roughly 120 kW. That is 5× the flash option's power, the regime where vendor claims of "75% less power" become plausible.

    3. NAND at hyperscaler cost. In Everpure's second hyperscaler deal the customer buys its own NAND28. The NAND then reaches the buyer near contract price, not array street price, which shrinks the media premium. That is why hyperscalers move first.

    Where disk still wins¶

    Disk wins cold data at rest: archives, backup copies and data lakes read rarely. There the bandwidth requirement is low and $/TB dominates. It also wins on embodied carbon. Tannu and Nair estimated about 0.16 kg CO2e per GB to manufacture flash, about 8× disk per unit of capacity24. In the example above, the embodied gap (~18.6 kt) dwarfs the operational savings (1.23 GWh, or 120–620 t at any grid intensity from 0.1 to 0.5 kg/kWh). That 2022 figure predates today's denser NAND, so treat it as an order of magnitude. Flash's carbon case leans on longer service life and on avoided racks and buildings.

    Everpure's structural edge is that it owns the flash translation layer, so its module capacity can run ahead of commodity SSDs. That edge narrows whenever a 245 TB drive ships (Chapter 26). The steelman for VAST and WEKA is that commodity 245 TB QLC brings much of the TB/W benefit with a broader supply base. The steelman for disk is the table above: at 2026 prices, cold exabytes stay on HDD. Options a strategy team might evaluate, based on public information: publishing audited system-level TB/W and GB/s per kW against NVIDIA reference sizings, and pricing guarantees per usable TiB for AI data that does not reduce.

    History and trajectory¶

    In 2021, flash vendors like VAST argued density per rack unit33. In 2023, Pure Storage added a watts-per-TiB SLA31 and showed roughly 1 W/TB with FlashBlade//E15. In 2024–25 power entered the CEO vocabulary: Zuckerberg4, Huang1, Jassy2. Meta published its QLC case27, widely read as pointing to DirectFlash, and Pure's hyperscaler revenue began. In 2026, 245 TB QLC drives shipped10 while NAND prices rose every quarter37. That widened the $/TB gap just as the power argument peaked.

    Disk is not standing still. Western Digital targets HAMR at 100 TB by 202919. At constant ~10 W per drive that is ~10 TB/W, about today's best QLC at the drive. Expect flash to answer with 512 TB-class drives and larger modules (Chapter 53), and the contest to shift from TB/W to GB/s per kW, where disk cannot follow.

    Conversation playbook¶

    Questions for a PM

    1. "In a power-capped AI deal, what is our system TB/W and GB/s per kW at the customer's real configuration, including compute?" Strong: a number with scope and watts basis stated, and a named competitor configuration.
    2. "Where do we lose to disk, and do we walk away?" Strong: cold archive and backup at 2026 NAND prices; a break-even $/TB premium per segment.
    3. "How does the watts-per-TiB guarantee behave on AI data that does not reduce?" Strong: the usable-capacity figure and who bears the risk.

    Questions for an engineer

    1. "What fraction of system power is NAND, and what is controller, DRAM, fans and NICs?" Strong: a breakdown showing where DFM design saves watts, not just "bigger modules".
    2. "What do our boxes draw idle, at read-mostly load and during rebuild?" Strong: measured curves, not nameplate.
    3. "How does QLC write power scale with sustained ingest?" Strong: watts per GB/s written, and how the write path limits it.

    Don't say this:

    • "Flash saves so much power it pays for itself." Not at grid prices: about $170K against tens of millions for 100 PB.
    • "Our TB/W beats disk," comparing a drive with an enclosure. Compare whole systems.
    • "HDD is dead." Disks are shipping at 40–44 TB, with HAMR targeting 100 TB.
    • "Storage power is a big share of an AI datacenter." It is a few percent; it matters only because the cap is hard.

    Going deeper (optional, L3 pointers)¶

    • Meta's QLC case and E2 form-factor work276: bandwidth per TB as the tiering criterion.
    • Tannu and Nair, "The Dirty Secret of SSDs"24: embodied-carbon method and its sensitivity to refresh cycles.
    • Chapter 48 for how NAND pricing moves the break-even.

    Key terms¶

    • Power cap (site) — the fixed grid allocation that bounds all IT load.
    • TB/W — capacity per watt; drive-level and system-level figures differ widely.
    • PB per rack — rack density, limited by power, cooling and floor loading.
    • GB/s per kW — bandwidth per kilowatt, the AI-era storage efficiency metric.
    • Bandwidth per TB — throughput a device delivers per unit of capacity; falls as disks grow.
    • HAMR — heat-assisted magnetic recording, disk's capacity roadmap.
    • Embodied carbon — emissions from manufacturing hardware.
    • Dennard scaling — the historical rule that kept power density constant as transistors shrank.
    • Powered shell — a datacenter building with grid power and cooling ready for IT.
