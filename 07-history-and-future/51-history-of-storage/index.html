    51. History of storage, 1956–2026¶

    Depth: L2 · Reading time: ~12 min · Last verified: 2026-11-01 Prerequisites: The storage stack · Scale-up dual-controller arrays · Disaggregated shared-everything · Everpure company history

    TL;DR¶

    • Seventy years, six eras: captive disk, arrays and networks, scale-out and cloud, flash, software-defined, and AI. Each was organized around the most expensive resource of its time: the disk itself, then administrators and stranded capacity, then scale, then latency, now GPU time.
    • Cost fell by nearly nine orders of magnitude. Disk went from about US$10,000 per MB (1956) to US$0.014 per GB (2022), about 26% a year. Flash fell faster. In 2026 both curves are rising.
    • New media creates new vendors, but only when software captures the media's economics. Pure Storage (now Everpure) survived independently by selling data reduction and a business model, not speed. Violin sold speed and filed for Chapter 11.
    • Incumbents rarely die. They get acquired, usually after buying their way into the new wave. EMC bought Isilon and XtremIO, then was itself bought by Dell for about US$67B in 2016.
    • Architecture swings like a pendulum between centralized and distributed designs, and software-defined waves recur whenever commodity CPUs and networks catch up with proprietary controllers. AI-era disaggregation is the latest swing, not the last.

    Why this matters for Everpure strategy¶

    Storage has run the same plot several times. A media or interconnect shift opens a gap, start-ups pour in, one or two independents survive, incumbents buy the rest, and the survivors become the next incumbents. Pure Storage was the flash wave's surviving independent. Everpure is now an incumbent being attacked by AI-native vendors (VAST, WEKA, a re-energized DDN) that were founded on the next shift. Product-only responses lose. Acquisitions buy time. Business-model and platform responses last longest.

    "Disaggregation", "software-defined" and "data platform" all have precedents, and so do the ways earlier waves ended. Chapter 52 covers flash cost curves in depth, Chapter 53 looks forward, and Chapter 46 profiles today's incumbents.

    Era 1, 1956–1987: the disk as a captive, costly peripheral¶

    IBM's 350 disk unit shipped with the 305 RAMAC in 1956. It held about 3.75 MB on fifty 24-inch platters1, and its cost worked out to roughly US$10,000 per MB23. Disk was the scarce resource, so systems were designed to waste no byte. In June 1980 IBM's 3380 became the first gigabyte-class drive. It held 2.52 GB, was the size of a refrigerator, and listed at US$81,000–142,4002, or about US$32,000 per GB at the low end (81,000 ÷ 2.52).

    The business model was the IDM model in semiconductors. IBM designed the heads and media, built the drives, owned the channel interface and sold the whole system. Margins were set by the system, not by the disk.

    Era 2, 1988–2005: arrays, cache and networks¶

    The break came from below. In 1988 Patterson, Gibson and Katz argued that many small PC disks, protected by parity, could beat one "single large expensive disk" on cost, performance and reliability3. Their Table I is the thesis in two numbers. An IBM 3380 cost US$18–10 per MB including controller, and a small Conners CP3100 cost US$10–73. Semiconductor bridge: this is the "killer micros" argument applied to storage, and it rests on the same economics that make chiplets attractive. Many small, cheap, high-volume units plus clever redundancy beat one large, expensive, low-volume unit.

    RAID turned the disk into a component and moved value into the controller: cache, parity, mirroring and later snapshots and replication. EMC's Symmetrix, launched in 1990, put a large cache in front of commodity disks and attached it to IBM mainframes4. It is the storage equivalent of putting SRAM in front of DRAM: most reads hit the cache, so cheap disks behind it look fast. By 1995, by one account, Symmetrix held 41% of mainframe disk terabytes against IBM's 35%24. A start-up selling software and cache beat the company that invented the disk.

    Networking then made arrays shareable. NetApp was founded in 1992 to build file-serving "appliances" that did nothing but serve NFS, and it shipped its first systems in 19935. Fibre Channel's physical standard was approved in 19946, which let many servers share one block array over a SAN. The economic driver was utilization and administration. Disks scattered across hundreds of servers strand capacity and need hundreds of backup jobs. Consolidating them behind two controllers fixes both problems. That is the dual-controller array of Chapter 14.

    Era 3, 2001–2012: scale-out and the cloud¶

    The next limit was the controller pair itself. Unstructured data outgrew any two-controller box. Isilon, founded in 2001, clustered x86 nodes behind a single file namespace. EMC bought it in 2010 for about US$2.25B7. Google published GFS in 200325, and Amazon launched S3 on 14 March 20068. By 2023 it held more than 280 trillion objects26. Nutanix shipped hyperconverged infrastructure (HCI) in 2011, which put the storage software on the same x86 servers as the virtual machines9 (Chapter 19).

    The shared enabler was cheap x86 cores and Ethernet. Once commodity servers could run erasure coding and metadata services in software, the proprietary controller stopped being necessary for scale. This was the first software-defined wave, and its biggest winner (AWS) was not a storage vendor at all.

    Era 4, 2007–2017: flash makes new vendors¶

    Flash attacked the remaining scarce resource, which was latency. A 15K RPM disk delivers a few hundred random IOPS. A single SSD delivers tens of thousands or more. Fusion-io unveiled its ioDrive PCIe card in 200711. A wave of array start-ups followed: Violin, Nimble, XtremIO and Pure Storage (incorporated October 2009, FlashArray shipped May 2012)12. The NVMe 1.0 specification, published in March 2011, replaced a disk-era command interface with one designed for parallel flash10.

    The cohort's outcomes are the most useful dataset in this chapter:

    • Fusion-io (server flash) was sold to SanDisk in 2014 for about US$1.1B27. A NAND maker integrated forward.
    • XtremIO was bought by EMC in May 2012, before it had any product revenue, for a reported US$430M13.
    • Nimble was bought by HPE in 2017 for US$1.09B15.
    • Violin, the early speed leader, IPO'd in 2013 and filed for Chapter 11 in December 201614.
    • Pure Storage stayed independent, IPO'd in 2015 and settled EMC's litigation for US$30M in 201628 (Chapter 25).

    The difference was not raw performance, because flash made every array fast. Pure used inline deduplication and compression to bring low-cost MLC flash close to the effective price of performance disk. It wrapped that in a non-disruptive upgrade subscription (Evergreen) that attacked incumbents' forklift-refresh revenue. Speed is a property of the media and gets commoditized. Economics and business model are properties of the software and the contract, and they compound. Chapter 52 explains why flash won primary storage.

    Era 5, 2014–2020: software-defined again, and the incumbent exits¶

    While flash start-ups fought for arrays, the buying centre moved. Developers on public cloud and Kubernetes wanted storage that came from an API, not a ticket. Pure bought Portworx, a Kubernetes data-services company, in September 2020 for about US$370M18. EMC, the defining array company of the 1990s and 2000s, had bought into each new wave (Isilon for scale-out, XtremIO for flash). Dell nonetheless agreed to buy it in October 2015 for about US$67B and closed the deal on 7 September 201616. Its products live on: Dell led the external storage market in 2Q26 with 23.8% share29. What disappeared was EMC's independence and its ability to set the agenda.

    Era 6, 2019–2026: AI and the return of throughput¶

    GPUs made a new resource the most expensive one in the data centre, so storage is now judged by how fully it keeps accelerators busy (Chapter 31). That favoured designs born for parallel throughput. VAST Data (founded 2016, out of stealth 2019) built disaggregated shared-everything17. WEKA (founded 2013) built a parallel file system in software30. DDN, founded in 1998 and long an HPC niche player, became an AI supplier. Blackstone invested US$300M at a US$5B valuation in January 202520, and DDN told Forbes it expected about US$1B of revenue in 2026 (reported by press)31. MLCommons published the first MLPerf Storage round in September 202319. VAST raised capital at a US$30B valuation in April 202622. In February 2026 Pure Storage renamed itself Everpure to signal a shift from flash arrays to data management21.

    The cost curve underneath every era¶

    Figure 51.2 — Nearly nine orders of magnitude of cost decline drove every architectural shift; 2026 is the first time both curves have turned up together. Data: data/ch51_cost_per_gb.csv

    Worked numbers. Disk went from about US$10,000,000 per GB in 1956 to US$0.0144 per GB in November 20222332. That is a factor of about 6.9 × 10⁸ over 66 years, so the annual factor is (6.9 × 10⁸)^(1/66) ≈ 1.36. Prices fell about 26% a year, in nominal dollars, before any inflation adjustment. Flash fell from about US$50,000 per GB (a 20 MB SunDisk drive at about US$1,000 in 1991)33 to US$0.185 per GB for enterprise SSD in Q2 202034. That works out to about 35% a year. The faster slope is what let flash overtake disk for primary storage. In 2026 the curves turned up: a single-vendor index put a 30 TB TLC SSD at US$753 per TB, 18.6× the per-TB price of a 30 TB nearline HDD35. The bases differ, so read the chart for direction and orders of magnitude, not decimals.

    Semiconductor bridge. The disk curve is storage's Moore's law. Like Moore's law, it changed architecture more than it changed products. Cheap capacity removed the need to economize on bytes, so designs shifted to economizing on whatever cost more: people (consolidation), scale (scale-out), latency (flash) and now accelerator time (AI).

    The architectural pendulum¶

    B -->|"stranded capacity, admin cost, SANs"| C["Centralized arrays<br/>1990s-2000s<br/>SAN and NAS"]
    C -->|"x86 clusters, Ethernet, web scale"| D["Distributed<br/>2000s-2010s<br/>scale-out, HCI, cloud"]

    Figure 51.3 — Storage swings between consolidating media for efficiency and spreading it for scale; each swing is triggered by whichever component (media, network or CPU) just got cheap.

    The swing has a mechanism. Centralize when the media is expensive and the network is cheap enough, because pooling raises utilization. Distribute when commodity compute is cheaper than the controller and the network becomes the bottleneck, because locality avoids the network. Disaggregate when the network becomes nearly as fast as local media. NVMe-oF adds roughly 5–15 µs to a ~100 µs flash read (Chapter 16), so it gives you pooling without giving up scale. The next swing is visible one level down, where CXL applies the same logic to memory (Chapter 12).

    Three recurring patterns, and the semiconductor parallel¶

    1. How incumbents lose: slowly, then by acquisition. No storage leader collapsed. Each lost its agenda first. IBM lost mainframe disk share to EMC's cache-and-software design. EMC bought into each new wave, but buying products did not change a business built on large, refresh-driven hardware sales. The end was a sale, not a failure. Media went the other way, to three survivors each. Micron paid US$615M for bankrupt Elpida's equity in 201336, and Samsung, SK hynix and Micron held about 90% of DRAM revenue in 4Q25 (36.0 + 32.1 + 22.4)37. Hard-disk makers narrowed to Seagate, Western Digital and Toshiba38.

    2. Why storage systems never consolidated like media. The capital intensity sits in the media: fabs and head/disk plants. Array vendors resemble fabless chip companies. They buy NAND or drives, add controller software and sell a system. Entry costs are venture rounds, not a multi-billion-dollar fab, so every technology shift produces a new crop of entrants. Everpure's DirectFlash strategy, which buys raw NAND and manages it in its own software, is like a fabless company designing its own memory controller. It captures media economics without owning the fab.

    3. Why software-defined waves recur. Whenever commodity CPUs and networks catch up with the proprietary controller, someone runs storage as software on standard servers: scale-out in the 2000s, HCI and cloud in the 2010s, Kubernetes-native storage and VAST's 2021 exit from hardware39 in the 2020s. These waves rarely displace arrays. Arrays absorb the model (subscriptions, cloud editions, container integration), while hyperscalers keep the pure-software prize. Implication for the AI era: expect the current AI-native leaders to face the same cycle. They will be validated, imitated by incumbents (NetApp AFX, HPE X10000 and Everpure EXA already ship disaggregated designs), and then consolidated or forced into platforms. The open question is who does the consolidating.

    Table 51.2 — Founding era predicts architecture; in the AI era the founding-era advantage belongs to VAST and WEKA, not Everpure.

    The honest read: Everpure sits where EMC sat around 2012, a successful incumbent of the previous wave with the cash and installed base to respond to the next one. EMC's responses were acquisitions on top of a hardware-refresh business. Everpure's are built in-house (EXA), subscription-led (Evergreen) and extended into hyperscale data centres by licensing DirectFlash (Meta in 2024, a second top-five hyperscaler in August 2026)46. That is closer to the business-model defence that let Pure itself survive in 2012–2016. The steelman for the challengers: VAST and WEKA did not retrofit anything, and the history of storage says architectural fit for the scarce resource usually beats installed base within one refresh cycle. Options a strategy team might evaluate, based on public information, include where EXA wins outright, which capabilities to buy rather than build (Portworx is the template), and how to avoid an EMC-style end state (Chapter 54).

    History and trajectory¶

    Three dated signals frame the next chapter of this history:

    • Costs reversed. Enterprise SSD went from US$185/TB (Q2 2020) to an index price of US$753/TB in Q3 2026, and nearline HDD from US$19/TB to about US$40/TB3435. Every earlier era assumed falling media prices; tight NAND supply rewards the data reduction Pure Storage built its first decade on (Chapter 48).
    • The market grew with the cycle. In 2Q26 external storage revenue reached US$10.3B (+33.6% YoY), and all-flash arrays were 52.1% of it. Everpure was #4 at 8.1% share and, at +50.0% YoY, the fastest grower among the top five vendors29.
    • Benchmarks and ecosystems became the gatekeepers. MLPerf Storage v3.0 had 19 submitters, 11 of them first-time, including Everpure47. As in the Fibre Channel and VMware eras, certification by the dominant platform (now NVIDIA) decides who gets considered (Chapter 34).

    Where it goes next, with CXL, KV-cache tiers and PLC NAND, is covered in Chapter 53.

    Conversation playbook¶

    Questions for a PM

    1. "Which part of our value proposition is media speed, and which is economics or business model?" A strong answer separates them, admits speed claims erode within a generation, and names the durable parts: reduction ratios, Evergreen renewal economics, power per TB.
    2. "What could make an AI-native architecture irrelevant, as flash commoditized Violin's speed lead?" A strong answer names a concrete shift (KV-cache moving to CXL memory, storage services on DPUs) and how the product would ride it.
    3. "Which past deal is our template, Portworx or EMC's XtremIO?" A strong answer judges integration, not price: did the technology join the platform or stay a separate product line?

    Questions for an engineer

    1. "Where does today's bottleneck sit: media, network, CPU or metadata?" A strong answer gives numbers (fabric µs versus flash µs, metadata operations per second) and which way that pushes the pendulum.
    2. "Which design decisions assume falling NAND prices?" Strong: over-provisioning, erasure-coding width, reduction assumptions, and what changes if $/TB rises for two years.
    3. "What from Purity's 2012 design would a 2026 clean-sheet design keep?" Strong: log-structured writes, global reduction, non-disruptive upgrades, plus what it would discard.

    Don't say this:

    • "Flash killed disk." Disk still stores most bytes; flash took primary storage (Chapter 52).
    • "EMC failed." It was sold for about US$67B, and its products still lead the market inside Dell.
    • "Disaggregation is new." SANs pooled storage decades ago; what is new is NVMe-oF making remote flash nearly local.
    • "Storage prices always fall." 2026 is a counter-example on both curves.
    • "Software-defined storage will replace arrays." Three waves have tried; arrays absorbed each one.

    Key terms¶

    • DASD — direct-access storage device, IBM's term for mainframe disk.
    • SLED — single large expensive disk, the RAID paper's foil.
    • Cached disk array — an array that fronts disks with a large RAM cache (EMC Symmetrix).
    • Filer / NAS appliance — a dedicated file server (NetApp).
    • SAN — storage area network, a block network such as Fibre Channel.
    • Architectural pendulum — the recurring swing between centralized, distributed and disaggregated storage.
    • Software-defined wave — a period when storage software moves onto commodity servers.
