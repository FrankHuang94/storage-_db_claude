    58. Explain it in 30 seconds¶

    Depth: L1 · Reading time: ~8 min · Last verified: 2026-11-01 Prerequisites: None; each line links to its source chapter.

    TL;DR¶

    • Use these 66 one-liners when a CFO or director asks "what is X, and why care?"
    • Four beats: what it is, why it matters, one number, what it means for us.
    • Quote only sourced, dated numbers; flag private-vendor figures as company-stated.
    • Keep the trade-off. "Flash costs ~18× more per terabyte yet wins on density" beats "flash is better."
    • Memorise the eight numbers at the end.

    Why this matters for Everpure strategy¶

    Corporate development carries the technology story to people who approve capital, and they give each concept half a minute. Everpure (NYSE: P, formerly PSTG) is hard to explain: its value sits in software (Purity), its own flash module (DirectFlash), a business model (Evergreen) and hyperscaler licensing, none visible from the hardware.

    Media and economics¶

    1. Flash vs disk. Chips versus spinning platters. Flash wins speed, power and space; disk wins cold-data price (Ch 2).
    2. Flash price premium. Enterprise SSDs cost ~18.6× disk per terabyte in Q3 2026, up from ~10× in 20201, so flash sells on density (Ch 53).
    3. NAND. The chip inside every SSD. Five suppliers earned $68.87B in 2Q262, and also sell competing drives (Ch 48).
    4. NAND cycle. Forecasts compound to ~4.7× mid-2025 contract prices by 4Q263. Revenue inflates first; margins recover later (Ch 48).
    5. QLC. Four bits per cell: cheapest flash, but less durable. Purity makes it work by writing large sequential chunks (Ch 2).
    6. Data reduction. Deduplication and compression multiply usable capacity. Purity averages ~5:14; AI data barely shrinks (Ch 5).
    7. Raw, usable, effective. Three prices: before protection, after it, and after assumed reduction. Compare like with like (Ch 4).
    8. Watts. AI sites are power-capped, so storage watts displace GPUs. A rack of the largest SSDs holds ~5× the petabytes of a disk rack, at 2–3× the power (Ch 38).

    How storage systems work¶

    1. Block, file, object. Blocks for databases, folders for people, keys for cloud and AI; each has its own buyer (Ch 1).
    2. Array. A computer that pools, protects and serves drives over a network; customers really pay for its software (Ch 13).
    3. Dual controller. Two servers share drives, so either can fail without downtime, as in FlashArray (Ch 14).
    4. Scale-out. Add boxes rather than enlarge one. It reaches petabytes but loads the network (Ch 15).
    5. Disaggregation. Buy engines and fuel tanks separately. VAST pioneered it; rivals now ship versions (Ch 16).
    6. Parallel file system. One lookup, then clients read from many servers at once. Lustre runs over 60% of the Top5005 (Ch 17).
    7. Metadata. The card catalogue: names, folders, permissions. AI's millions of small files make it the usual bottleneck (Ch 21).
    8. Erasure coding. Parity math that survives drive failures: eight-plus-two costs 25% extra capacity, a mirror 100% (Ch 6).
    9. Immutable snapshots. Copies even an administrator cannot delete, so ransomware victims restore instead of paying (Ch 6).
    10. Synchronous replication. Every write lands at two sites first, so a site loss loses nothing; distance limits it to metros (Ch 6).
    11. "Eleven nines." A modelled durability figure, not a measurement. Correlated failures are what actually lose data (Ch 20).
    12. Non-disruptive upgrade. Swapping controllers while data keeps flowing: the engineering behind Evergreen (Ch 14).
    13. Control plane. Fleet-management software. More systems reporting telemetry predict more failures, so the moat compounds (Ch 22).

    Protocols and networks¶

    1. Protocol. The language servers and storage speak; it decides which buyers a product reaches (Ch 7).
    2. NVMe. The protocol built for flash, with thousands of parallel queues. It replaced SCSI as the speed standard (Ch 7).
    3. NVMe over Fabrics. NVMe across a network, so shared flash feels local; the TCP version is becoming the default (Ch 7).
    4. NFS and pNFS. NFS is Linux's file language; pNFS reads many servers in parallel. FlashBlade//EXA uses it to avoid special clients (Ch 8).
    5. S3. Amazon's object interface, now the standard. AWS S3 holds over 500 trillion objects6 (Ch 9).
    6. RDMA. Network cards copying memory between machines, skipping the CPU, to keep GPUs fed (Ch 10).
    7. Ethernet vs InfiniBand. Ethernet took over two-thirds of AI back-end switch sales in 20257, favouring vendors built on standard networks (Ch 10).
    8. DPU. A network card that runs storage software. NVIDIA's BlueField-4 STX named 12 storage partners, Everpure included8 (Ch 34).

    AI and storage¶

    1. Storage for AI. Keeping GPUs busy. At about $2–6 per GPU-hour910, an idle 1,024-GPU cluster burns ~$0.6–1.7 a second (Ch 11).
    2. "AI storage." At least eight workloads, from bulk ingest to inference. Ask vendors which stage they actually win (Ch 30).
    3. Checkpoint. Saving the game. A 405B-parameter model's state is ~6.5 TB; saving it in a minute needs ~108 GB/s (Ch 31).
    4. Failure rate. Meta's Llama 3 run had 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days11, so fast restores protect GPU spend (Ch 31).
    5. GPUDirect Storage. Data straight into GPU memory; nearly every vendor claims it, so it no longer differentiates (Ch 11).
    6. KV cache. The AI's short-term memory: ~43 GB per long session on a 70B model12, so fleets reach petabytes (Ch 32).
    7. Context memory. NVIDIA's new flash tier between GPU memory and shared storage; every vendor wants to supply it (Ch 53).
    8. RAG. AI looking up company documents before answering, turning storage into search and governance (Ch 33).
    9. Agents and MCP. Agents need findable, permitted data. MCP passed 10,000 servers13, turning metadata into a product (Ch 39).
    10. NVIDIA certification. NVIDIA's approved-vendor list: absence usually means no deal, but it proves a floor, not a ranking (Ch 34).
    11. Neocloud. A GPU-specialist cloud; CoreWeave guides 2026 capex to $35–39B14. The largest disclosed storage contract is about 1% a year of a neocloud's capex, yet storage can idle every GPU (Ch 35).
    12. MLPerf Storage. A peer-reviewed benchmark with simulated GPUs. EXA led v3.0 checkpoint writes at 877.5 GiB/s15 (read was 588 GiB/s); VAST, WEKA and DDN skipped the round16 (Ch 37).

    Everpure's model¶

    1. FlashArray and FlashBlade. Block for databases and VMs; file and object at scale. Both run Purity (Ch 27).
    2. Purity. Everpure's storage operating system. It writes like a database log, which makes cheap flash last (Ch 26).
    3. DirectFlash. Raw flash modules run by Purity, without SSD controllers. Modules reach 300 TB17 versus 245.76 TB for standard SSDs18 (Ch 26).
    4. Evergreen. Hardware refreshes in place, without migration; over 10,000 such upgrades by 202219 anchor retention (Ch 23).
    5. Evergreen//One. Storage on a utility bill; Everpure owns the hardware. It lowers reported growth while raising contracted revenue (Ch 23).
    6. ARR and RPO. Subscription ARR $2.13B (+20%) and RPO $4.1B (+44%) at Q2 FY2720: the recurring base under the hardware (Ch 29).
    7. Hyperscaler licensing. Hyperscalers license DirectFlash and buy their own NAND. Meta passed 2 EB in FY2621; a second signed August 202617 (Ch 29).
    8. FlashBlade//EXA. Purity runs metadata, third-party servers hold data, clients use standard pNFS: Everpure's AI-scale answer to VAST and WEKA (Ch 27).
    9. Pure1 and Fusion. Fleet analytics and policy control. Fusion passed 1,200 customers22, moving switching costs into software (Ch 28).
    10. Portworx and 1touch. Kubernetes storage, and data classification bought for $125.0M23. Both bet on selling software above the array (Ch 28).
    11. Margin squeeze. Q2 FY27 non-GAAP product margin was 66.2% versus 74.9% for subscription24; strategic NAND and component purchases made Q2 free cash flow −$238M25 (Ch 29).
    12. The rename. A bid for a data-platform valuation while revenue is still mostly arrays (Ch 25).

    Competition¶

    1. Market share. In 2Q26: Dell 23.8%, Huawei 11.3%, NetApp 9.6%, Everpure 8.1%, HPE 6.8%26. One share point is a big year (Ch 40).
    2. Incumbents. Dell, NetApp and HPE win on installed base, channel and server bundles. Their weakness is pre-AI software estates (Ch 46).
    3. VAST. The AI-native leader, valued at $30B27 on over $500M committed ARR (company-stated)28. Its $1.17B CoreWeave deal29 anchors the segment (Ch 42).
    4. WEKA. Software-only parallel file system, last valued at $1.6B in 202430. Early in KV cache, smaller than its profile (Ch 43).
    5. DDN. The Lustre incumbent inside NVIDIA's own supercomputers; Blackstone invested at a $5B valuation31 (Ch 44).
    6. Hyperscaler self-build. At their volume, building beats buying arrays; they buy parts and IP, Everpure's licensing seam (Ch 36).
    7. Hammerspace. A namespace layer above everyone's arrays. If it wins, arrays look interchangeable (Ch 47).

    Market and financial dynamics¶

    1. Market size. IDC's external storage market was $33.0B in 202532, branded arrays only; hyperscaler self-builds sit outside (Ch 40).
    2. Price-led growth. The market grew 33.6% in 2Q2633, mostly flash costs passing through, not more capacity (Ch 40).
    3. AI storage TAM. No credible figure. Storage is 2.4% of IDC's AI infrastructure spend34; the atlas estimates ~$9–25B for 2026 (Ch 40).

    Future¶

    1. Bigger drives. 512 TB SSDs are on 2027 roadmaps35. Rebuild time and watts become the limits (Ch 53).
    2. Disk's future. Not dying: Western Digital targets 100 TB drives by 202936. Disk keeps cold bulk (Ch 53).
    3. Where value moves. Up into data services and down into the network; the box matters less (Ch 53).

    Conversation playbook¶

    Three questions for a PM

    1. "Explain your product to our CFO in one sentence." Strong: buyer, problem and one dated number, with no adjectives.
    2. "Which board question worries you most?" Strong: names a real gap, such as EXA's pending NCP certification, and the plan.
    3. "Which number belongs on one slide?" Strong: a customer outcome, not a peak benchmark.

    Three questions for an engineer

    1. "What do skeptics get wrong about DirectFlash?" Strong: system-level, not datasheet, gains.
    2. "Where does my disaggregation one-liner break?" Strong: fabric and minimum size.
    3. "Is the KV-cache opportunity storage or memory?" Strong: only cache not being decoded can leave GPU memory, so it is a bandwidth tier.

    Don't say this:

    • "Flash is cheaper than disk." Not per raw terabyte in 2026.
    • "We're #1 in MLPerf." Say first among those who entered.
    • "AI storage is a $50B market." Nothing credible supports that.
    • "Evergreen means free upgrades." It is a paid subscription.
